# Weeping Somnambulist: The Architects

## I. Who They Were

The Architects were a Victorian-era research organization operating in Budapest during the Austro-Hungarian golden age. Their core purpose was intellectual: study the Deep, build infrastructure to go deeper, formalize what they learned. The Cistern, the heptagonal geometry, the Underworks as a whole — these are an intellectual project, not a commercial one.

The Mordaunt family brought the extraction craft and the institutional backing. The Architects provided depth access and the research framework. The espionage capability the Mordaunts carried was their own tradition; the Architects documented findings the Mordaunts could apply, without that being their primary orientation.

They were not naive Victorian gentlemen stumbling onto something inexplicable. By the time they built the Institute they had years of pre-surface-building practice — skilled practitioners doing significant work before 1887, before the Eye, before the collapse.

---

## II. The Site

The Budapest site has been occupied continuously since Roman times. The Architects did not choose a blank lot. They chose a place that had already accumulated significance across centuries:

- **Roman:** The Romans found a natural karst fissure during bath construction. 20–30m of accessible void, already present in the geology. They shored it up, used it for drainage, stopped when the water below behaved strangely — wrong temperature, unexplained current. The fissure became a feature of the bath complex rather than an investigation.
- **Ottoman:** The hammam period relined and stabilized the existing Roman shaft. The lower section was sealed off as unusable — too cold, too strange. The Ottomans' contribution is masonry improvement and documentation, not depth. The shaft reaches the same 30–35m it always did, but is better built.
- **The Architects:** Found all of it, understood what it was, and built the Institute around and above it.

The spring at the Cistern's heart is the same feature the Romans built baths around. The Architects didn't install a pool. They built around something that was already there.

### What Previous Occupants Experienced

The site produces discharge effects when the local psychosphere moves — pressure across dissimilar materials generating something like a piezoelectric spark. The conditions are ideal: Roman limestone, Ottoman brick and mortar, Architect-cut stone and brass fittings, all bonded imperfectly at the joins, all with different resonance properties. A psychosphere storm moving through the site doesn't pass through uniform material. It passes through a stack of incompatible substrates and finds its own path.

The effect is not consistent. The same pressure produces different expression each time, depending on where it breaks through: a flash at a Roman/Ottoman join, a smell of ozone in the older corridors, a low tone that isn't quite sound, a crack along a line of Ottoman tile that has cracked there before. Stress-relieving in the same places it always has.

**The Romans** were systematic about prodigia — unexpected natural phenomena that required interpretation and formal response. A flash of light from the bath floor, heat in the stone during certain seasons, the water in the lower cistern running against its nature — these would have been recorded, reported, and treated as requiring consultation. Possibly offerings. The site accumulating religious significance across Roman tenure is not superstition; it's methodology applied to phenomena that resisted other explanation. The Romans kept coming back because the site kept producing things they couldn't resolve and couldn't ignore.

**The Ottomans** read it architecturally. Something wrong with the materials, a geological instability, a reason to seal the lower section and route around it rather than investigate. Ottoman builders were pragmatic about persistent anomalies: you don't repair what you can contain. The sealed lower shaft is their answer — not an investigation, a boundary.

What this means for the Architects: every repair, every rerouting, every sealed section left by previous occupants is a response to a question they didn't know they were asking. The Architects would have read the layers as a partial map — not of construction history but of the Deep's behavior at this node over centuries. Where the Romans put offerings, where the Ottomans put mortar: that's a record of where the pressure came through.

## III. The Plumb: Why It Exists and How It Was Built

### The depth problem

The shaft's listed depth is 300 meters. This figure is accurate and misleading simultaneously.

The Architects dug from ~35m of existing Roman/Ottoman shaft to approximately **90m physical depth** — a serious Victorian engineering achievement requiring months of work, significant machinery, and sustained funding. The remaining 210m is not geological. It is psychospheric: a property of the Deep's relationship with the site rather than the distance drilled.

The Architects' instruments — calibrated to the Deep rather than to surveying standards — measured the descent as 300m. A plumb line dropped from the surface measures one thing. Their instruments measure another. The discrepancy was documented, argued over, and eventually accepted as an operational fact. No one produced a clean explanation. The Antechamber sits at the bottom of 90m of rock and approximately 210m of something else.

Attempts to triangulate the Antechamber's location from the surface using conventional surveying do not land where they should.

### Targeted excavation

The Architects did not dig blind. They had prior dive experience through the natural fissure — years of descent before the shaft existed, using the fissure's irregular access. They knew approximately where the Antechamber boundary was. The Plumb is precision infrastructure built toward a known target, not exploratory mining.

The psychosphere provided directional intelligence. Not instructions — the Deep does not communicate in instructions — but accumulated dive experience told the Architects which way to go and roughly how far. The shaft's geometry (octagonal; the geometry of bridging earth and spirit) reflects the Architects' understanding of what they were building toward, not just engineering preference.

### The funding problem

A 90m urban shaft in 1880s Budapest required: industrial pumps, winding gear, timber lining, months of skilled labor, and permits. The Architects could not self-fund this as gentlemen-scholars. They needed patrons, and the only patrons with sufficient resources to ask no questions were patrons with reasons of their own not to ask questions.

Austro-Hungarian military and intelligence interests would have found a secret underground complex highly attractive. Private societies with esoteric orientations were active in the period and well-capitalized. Each funding source came with strings: oversight, questions the Architects couldn't fully answer, obligations they couldn't publicly acknowledge.

To maintain control, they obscured what they were building. Permits filed as drainage improvements, structural reinforcement, private cistern work. The deception was not malicious — it was the only way to complete the project. But it created a documentation problem: the Architects' official records of the shaft's construction are incomplete by design.

### The attention problem

The natural fissure attracted low-level psychospheric attention — the site's accumulated significance drew drifters, phenomena, ambient resonance. A 90m dig with industrial machinery, a rotating labor force, and an increasing population of Architects running regular dives was something categorically different.

Whatever inhabits the Deep became aware that something persistent and intentional was happening at this surface node. The Architects began attracting phenomena before the Plumb was finished — which may explain the timing of the 1887 collapse. They completed the infrastructure and immediately faced a crisis that the infrastructure alone could not have produced. The Plumb opened a channel the Deep had been pressing against.

## IV. The 1887 Collapse

The collapse was not sudden. It was a slow erosion — what the records call the Long Fade.

After capturing the Eye fragment (believing they had harpooned a Leviathan, not knowing what they actually held), the Architects began suffering Lethe-Light exposure. First came the forgetting of names, then functions, then humanity. They drifted while still awake. The Ghost Floors the crew experiences during storm surges replay this era: spectral Architects wandering the catwalks, staring at tools they no longer recognize, repeating maintenance tasks because they forgot they just finished.

The pre-Eye knowledge layer is the hardest to recover precisely because by the time the Architects were writing comprehensively, the Eye had been running for years and pre-Eye practice was assumed. It's in the oldest artifacts, in Barnaby, in the Architect memories surfacing through Ren's TSG connection — and those memories are cleaner because the dissolution didn't start until after the capture.

---

## V. The Funding Tail

The knowledge that the shaft exists did not die with the Architects. Whoever backed the dig — patrons, state actors, private societies — inherited a claim. After 1887 that knowledge went quiet, was passed down, sold, lost, rediscovered. By the story's present day there may be parties with an institutional or financial claim on the Institute who have no precise understanding of what they hold a claim on.

This is distinct from active antagonists. It is a liability in the background: documentation that references the site in terms a current owner might investigate, if the right archival thread were pulled.

---

## VI. What They Left

- The Institute building, purpose-built in the 1880s
- The Underworks: Antechamber, Cistern, Vault, Dive Room, catwalks, the Plumb
- Artifacts in various states of integration
- Heavily documented post-Eye practice; sparse pre-Eye records
- Barnaby — the only living index of what was here before 1887
- The shaft itself, with its layered predecessors still legible in the stone: Roman masonry at the upper levels, Ottoman lining through the middle, Architect precision work from ~40m down
- A funding trail that has not fully gone cold
