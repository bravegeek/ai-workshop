# Zoltán Kovács

**Full name:** Kovács Zoltán — Hungarian order, family name first. He uses the Western order with the crew without being asked. Old courtesy.
**Role:** Groundskeeper. Caretaker. The person who keeps the building alive.
**Origin:** Budapest, born on the campus. His father was born on the campus. His grandfather was born on the campus. His great-great-grandfather was hired by the Architects in 1884 to help build it.

---

## The Family

Four generations of Kovácses have maintained this building. The first arrived when the foundation was being dug — a young laborer from the VIII. district who stayed because the work kept extending and then because he was asked to stay on permanently. He was good with his hands and he didn't ask questions about the cistern. That second quality mattered as much as the first.

He passed the job to his son, who passed it to his son. The knowledge accumulated the way it does in families — through demonstration, through story, through the specific grammar of a particular building. Which pipes freeze first. Which windows need the secondary latch in wind. Where the ground settles in spring and which flagstones shift. How the basement changes pressure before a storm, and which of those pressure changes are about the weather.

Zoltán's grandfather is the one with the most stories. He was a boy when the Architects were still here — old enough to have impressions, young enough that the impressions didn't organize into questions until later. He described them as *men who worked at night and slept at odd hours and were always very polite and sometimes you couldn't find them anywhere in the building even when you knew they were inside.* He described the dog. He described what it was like when they were gone — how the building changed quality, like a room after a party clears. Present but empty.

Zoltán grew up on these stories. He doesn't discount them. He also doesn't need them to explain anything to himself. He knows the building. That's sufficient.

---

## The Dormant Period

After the Architects dispersed, the property held through a legal trust — distant, quarterly correspondence from a law firm in Vienna, maintenance funds released reliably, no instructions beyond *keep it standing.* The Kovács family did exactly that. Three generations of keeping it standing through two world wars, Soviet occupation, the whole weight of the twentieth century. The building survived. The family stayed.

Forty years ago, Zoltán's father — practical man, no income beyond maintenance funds — approached the law firm about letting the upper floors. The firm took six months to respond and said yes. A small music conservatory moved in. Children from the neighborhood learning violin, piano, voice. The building changed again — back toward occupied, toward warm. Zoltán's father said it felt like the building was relieved.

The school ran for thirty-seven years. When IO arrived and the operation established itself, there was a conversation. The school was displaced gently — IO found them a space three streets over, paid the first year's rent without being asked. The groundskeeper's family noticed that. It wasn't nothing.

Some of the children who learned violin in those upper rooms are adults now. They remember the building. They remember the dog, though none of them could say exactly what breed. They have their own folklore about the place. Budapest is a city that keeps its stories.

## Zoltán Himself

Fifties. Broad through the shoulders, economical in movement. His hands are permanently calloused in specific places that map to specific tools. He wears the same kind of clothes his father wore — practical, durable, not quite a uniform but consistent enough to read as one.

He is not superstitious in any way he would name. He has practices. Things the family has always done: the threshold of the east corridor gets swept before any work done in the building, not after. The cistern room door is left open on the first of each month. Certain areas of the basement he enters only when there's a reason, and he always announces himself before entering. These are habits, not rituals. The distinction matters to him.

He knows something significant is happening in the lower levels. He's known since IO arrived. He has not asked what it is and does not intend to. His job is the building. The building is standing. That's the job.

He watches IO sometimes — not openly — the way you watch someone carrying something heavy to see if they need help without offering before they want it. He doesn't know what she's carrying. He knows the weight is real.

---

## The Building as Knowledge

He knows this building the way very few people know anything. Not the operational knowledge IO has — the logistics, the ley lines, the Vault's semantic architecture. The physical knowledge. Where the stress cracks are, which ones are moving and which ones stopped decades ago. How the stone breathes in summer. The specific sound the basement makes under certain atmospheric pressures, which are not always the pressures that appear on weather instruments.

He has found things over the years. Artifacts in the walls during repair work. Rooms that weren't on any plan he had. A passage in the east wing that closes seasonally — open in winter, sealed by something in summer. He doesn't discuss these with IO. They are the building's, not his to give. If she needed to know, she'd ask.

He has been standing very still near the stairwell off the second landing for thirty years, occasionally, listening to something. He has not identified what he's listening for. He knows when the quality changes.

## His Family

**His wife, Erzsébet** — sharp, practical, fond of the crew in a way she expresses through feeding them when they surface looking wrong. She ran the school relationship for years: the scheduling, the families, the small politics of a neighborhood institution. She has better social intelligence than Zoltán and a more explicit framework for the strangeness — she grew up Catholic, and while she doesn't map the building directly onto that, she has categories. She lights a candle on certain dates. She doesn't explain this.

**The eldest, Dóra** — twenty-three. Born on campus, raised on campus, knows the building the way her father does and then some. She was the child who found the passage in the east wing. She was eleven. She told her father and then they both agreed not to mention it to anyone. She's studying architecture in Budapest and comes home on weekends. She and Ren have had exactly two conversations, both of which lasted longer than either of them intended.

**The middle child, Bence** — sixteen. At the age where he knows something significant is happening and doesn't have adult language for it yet. He's done his own investigation — quietly, the way his father does things quietly — and has a notebook. The notebook is meticulous: dates, times, locations, cross-referenced observations, and a strict right-margin column for things he cannot yet categorize. The right-margin column is often the longest. He is not getting things wrong — he is building a data set without a framework. When he eventually gets the framework, the data will still be good. He and Barnaby have an understanding. He's the only person on campus Barnaby plays with, in the limited way Barnaby plays.

**The youngest, Lili** — nine. Treats Barnaby as a dog. Treats the building as home. Has not yet organized the strangeness into a category requiring explanation. She leaves food out for Barnaby with the complete confidence that he eats it. Whether Barnaby eats it is an open question. The food disappears.

Nia taught ear training at the school on Tuesday afternoons — "creative listening," she called it. She'd been doing it since March before IO found out. All three children were in the class at some point. Lili still hums things she learned there.

## Relationship with the Crew

**IO:** Mutual respect with specific weight. She inherited a legal title to a building his family has maintained for four generations. They both know this and neither mentions it. She doesn't condescend. He doesn't perform deference. When she thanks him for something, she means it specifically, and he receives it the same way. He has caught her, twice, standing in the courtyard at 3am doing nothing in particular, and both times he left her alone. She noticed that he noticed and left her alone. That exchange established something between them that has no name.

When the school's lease came up for renewal, he had been prepared for the outcome. New operation, artifacts coming in, children in the building — he'd understood the logic even before she articulated it. Then nothing happened. The school stayed. He assumed it was IO's decision. He has credited her with it ever since — the owner who looked at his family and the children and chose decency over efficiency. That's a significant part of why his respect for IO carries the weight it does.

It was Nia's decision. IO has never corrected the impression. It's easier to carry the credit than to explain whose it actually was — and some part of her may not want to say it out loud.

**Ren:** She asked him a very precise question about the building's pressure behavior in year two of the operation. He answered it completely and accurately. She came back with a follow-up three days later. He answered that too. They have a working relationship based on the shared understanding that exact observations matter. He finds her unsentimental quality familiar.

**Lorcan:** They drink together occasionally, in the kitchen, late. Neither of them sleeps much. They don't talk about the Deep or the warden tradition. They talk about Budapest, about what the city was and what it's becoming, about the specific grief of watching something old be rationalized into something legible. Lorcan understands being the last person who remembers what something meant. Zoltán has not articulated this about himself, but Lorcan has.

**Ama:** She helped Erzsébet plant the courtyard garden. She is welcome at the family table whenever she wants. Lili follows her around. Ama has never asked about the building's strangeness directly, which Zoltán respects. She just exists in the space without extracting from it. He reads this correctly.

**Barnaby:** Barnaby was here before Zoltán was born. His grandfather described him. The dog has not changed. Zoltán treats this as a fact about the building rather than a mystery requiring solution. He makes sure Barnaby has access to the areas he prefers. He does not know what Barnaby is. He knows what Barnaby does — watches, stays close, goes still before things happen. That's enough to know.

---

## What He Carries

The weight of being the person the building is given to. Not owned — given. The Kovács family and this building have a relationship that predates the legal structure IO holds. He's not resentful of IO's ownership. He just knows it's not the whole story.

He wonders, sometimes, what his great-great-grandfather understood. The man left no written record — letters to his wife but nothing about the work. Family stories say he was a quiet man who came home on Sundays and slept in a way that suggested the week had been heavy. He stayed for forty years. He could have left.

Zoltán has never seriously considered leaving.

## Open Questions

- Dóra and the east wing passage: does she go back? Does she find something, eventually, that she brings to IO or Ren?
- Bence's notebook: the observations are meticulous and uncorrupted by practitioner frameworks. What does Ren do with it when she finally reads it? What does the right-margin column add up to?
- The seasonal passage in the east wing: what is it, and does it connect to anything the crew needs?
- Whether Zoltán ever asks, or is ever told, what is actually happening in the basement — and what he does with that knowledge.
